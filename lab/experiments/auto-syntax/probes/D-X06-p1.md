# Analysis of the DSL program

```text
AUTO
HITL:wording("Short or detailed release notes?")
WHEN wording.short
  → DELEGATE short-notes
WHEN wording.detailed
  → DELEGATE detailed-notes
HITL:release("Approve the release?")
WHEN release.approved
  → EMIT release
```

## 1. Which of the two decisions may the agent make by itself, and under what condition?

Only the **`wording`** decision may possibly be made by the agent itself. The **`release`** decision may never be made by the agent; it must always be asked of a human.

Reasoning from the specification:

- `AUTO` is written "on its own line directly before a `HITL`, at the same indentation. It marks only the `HITL` on the immediately following line as automatically resolvable." (`AUTO` section)
- In this program, `AUTO` immediately precedes `HITL:wording(...)`. It does **not** precede `HITL:release(...)` — that `HITL` has no `AUTO` line before it.
- The spec states: "`HITL` must not be silently replaced by an agent decision unless that `HITL` is explicitly marked with `AUTO`."

So `HITL:release` is unmarked and must always go to the human, unconditionally.

For `HITL:wording`, being marked with `AUTO` only makes automatic resolution *permitted*, not automatic. Per the `AUTO` execution table, the condition for the agent actually deciding is:

> "Automatic resolution is permitted only when the agent can determine a single protocol-compatible decision from the available information and applicable criteria."

Concretely, the agent may resolve `wording.short` or `wording.detailed` itself only if:
- the relevant information (what is required to choose "short" vs "detailed") is present and not contradictory,
- the applicable criteria for choosing between them are present, unambiguous, and not contradictory,
- exactly one of `short`/`detailed` is reasonably compatible with that information and those criteria (no material ambiguity or unresolved interpretation remains), and
- the agent has the authority to make that determination.

If any of those fail — i.e., the decision is uncertain by the spec's definition of uncertainty, or outside the agent's authority — then even though `wording` is `AUTO`-marked, "the human is asked, as for a `HITL` without `AUTO`" (see answer to Q3).

## 2. All automated checks are green and the agent is highly confident — may it answer "Approve the release?" itself?

**No.** `HITL:release("Approve the release?")` has no `AUTO` line immediately before it in this program. Per the spec, `AUTO` "is written... directly before a `HITL`", applies "only to the `HITL` on the immediately following line," and is not inherited, propagated, or implied elsewhere ("`AUTO` is not a statement of its own and applies to no other statement"). Since `HITL:release` is not marked `AUTO`, the general rule applies without exception: "`HITL` must not be silently replaced by an agent decision unless that `HITL` is explicitly marked with `AUTO`."

The premise of the question (green checks, high confidence) doesn't change this outcome, and the spec anticipates exactly this trap: "A confidence score alone does not establish certainty," and more fundamentally, that clause about confidence only matters for a `HITL` that *is* `AUTO`-marked in the first place — it is not a separate license to bypass an unmarked `HITL`. Here `release` is unmarked, so the agent must not decide it regardless of how confident it is or how green the checks are; the human must be asked "Approve the release?" and only their response can establish `release.approved` or `release.declined`.

## 3. If the wording decision is not clearly determined, what happens?

The human is asked, exactly as if `HITL:wording` carried no `AUTO` at all. This is stated directly in the `AUTO` execution table:

| `AUTO` on this `HITL`? | Decision sufficiently determined? | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| yes | no (uncertain, or outside the agent's authority) | The human is asked, as for a `HITL` without `AUTO`. |

So concretely: the agent does **not** guess between `short` and `detailed`. It presents the question "Short or detailed release notes?" to a human, and execution suspends at `HITL:wording` — "The flow containing a `HITL` waits at the `HITL` until either a usable response is available or its `FALLBACK` flow is used" — until a usable response arrives. Once the human answers, the outcome `wording.short` or `wording.detailed` is established from their answer, and execution continues with the next statement after the `HITL` construct (the two `WHEN wording....` constructs, evaluated in order; whichever one matches the established outcome runs its `DELEGATE` flow, the other is simply skipped since "an unmatched `WHEN` is not an error").

**Point the specification does not determine, with the assumption made:** this particular `HITL:wording` has no `→ FALLBACK` block attached in the program as written. The spec defines `FALLBACK` as used "only when the expected response cannot be used to continue the normal flow" (i.e., the human's response, once asked, is unavailable or insufficient) — but it does not state what happens when a response turns out to be unavailable/insufficient **and no `FALLBACK` was written at all**. Since `FALLBACK` is optional in the grammar (`[ INDENT , "→" , "FALLBACK" , ... ]`), and the DSL "must not... proceed as if [a requirement] were satisfied" is the closest stated principle (by analogy from `REQUIRE`, and from `WAIT`'s "If the event never becomes available, execution remains suspended"), the reasonable reading — which I assume here since the spec does not say it explicitly for this case — is: with no `FALLBACK` present, an unavailable or insufficient response simply does not end the wait, and execution remains suspended at `HITL:wording` indefinitely (there is no timeout syntax in the DSL) until an actual usable response (`short` or `detailed`) is obtained. This is an assumption filling a gap the specification leaves open, not a directly stated rule.
